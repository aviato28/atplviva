# 3. CANPA / CDFA
== The problem with old-style NPAs
Old non-precision approaches had **step-down fixes** inside the FAF. Flying them level-by-level means lots of thrust, pitch and altitude changes at low height = **high workload and error risk** in a critical phase.
Even without step-downs, the old way was "dive to MDA, then fly level at MDA until the runway appears or you reach the MAP" - a *dive-and-drive*.
== The fix: CANPA / CDFA
**CANPA** = Constant Angle Non-Precision Approach: fly a **stabilised constant-angle descent** even without a glide slope or VASI/PAPI.
**CDFA** = Continuous Descent Final Approach: continuous descent, **no level-off**, from at/above the FAF to about **15 m (50 ft) above the landing threshold**.
At the MDA you only have two options: **(1) continue below MDA with required visual reference, or (2) go around.** No level segment.
== Stabilised approach
Key to safe approach and landing; DGCA requires it to help eliminate **CFIT**.
Stable approach speed, descent rate, vertical flight path and configuration to the touchdown point. Leave the FAF **configured for landing**, on speed, power and flight path *before* descending below the minimum stabilised approach height.
== Advantages of CDFA (list of 5)
(a) Increased safety through stabilised approach. (b) Better situational awareness, lower workload. (c) Fuel efficiency - less low-level flight. (d) Lower noise - less time level at high thrust. (e) Reduced CFIT.
== Numbers
Optimum VDA (vertical descent angle) = **3.0 degrees**, calculated from FAF altitude to threshold crossing height (TCH).
Max VDA: **4.5 deg for Cat A/B**, **3.77 deg for Cat C/D**.
**Derived Decision Altitude (DDA)**: crew add an increment (min **50 ft**, depends on type) to the MDA to decide where to start the missed approach so they don't *descend below MDA* during the go-around transition.
If NOT CDFA: visibility penalty **200 m (A/B)**, **400 m (C/D)**.
Q: What is CANPA?
A: Constant Angle Non-Precision Approach - a stabilised constant-angle descent flown even when no glide slope or VASI/PAPI exists, instead of diving to MDA and levelling off.
Q: Define CDFA.
A: A technique, consistent with stabilised approach, for flying the final approach segment of an NPA as a continuous descent without level-off, from at/above the FAF to about 15 m (50 ft) above the landing threshold.
Q: What are the advantages of CDFA?
A: Safer (stabilised approach), better situational awareness and less workload, fuel saving, less noise, reduced CFIT.
T: S-S-F-N-C: Safety, Situational awareness, Fuel, Noise, CFIT.
QN: What's the optimum VDA and the maximum?
A: Optimum 3.0 deg. Max 4.5 deg for Cat A/B, 3.77 deg for Cat C/D. VDA is from FAF altitude to TCH.
Q: What is DDA?
A: Derived Decision Altitude - MDA plus a prescribed increment (min 50 ft, depending on aircraft type) at which the missed approach is initiated, so the aircraft doesn't descend below MDA.
Q: At MDA in a CDFA, what are your options?
A: Only two: continue below MDA to land with required visual reference, or go around. No level flight.
Q: What is a stabilised approach?
A: Maintaining stable approach speed, descent rate, vertical flight path and configuration to the touchdown point; configured for landing and on speed/power/path before descending below the minimum stabilised approach height. Required by DGCA to eliminate CFIT.
Q: What is TCH?
A: Threshold Crossing Height - the height of the effective visual glide path over the threshold.
Q: Domestic routes on Jeppesen enroute chart?
A: H, V, J, W are domestic routes; the "HV" ones are one-way routes.

# 4. Airspaces
== Seven classes - the ladder of service
Think of it as a ladder from "strictest control" (A) to "you're on your own" (G).
**A**: IFR only. All flights get ATC and are separated from each other.
**B**: IFR + VFR. All get ATC, all separated from each other.
**C**: IFR + VFR. All get ATC. IFR separated from IFR *and* VFR. VFR separated from IFR only, gets traffic info on other VFR.
**D**: IFR + VFR, all get ATC. IFR separated from IFR, gets traffic info on VFR. VFR gets traffic info on ALL.
**E**: IFR + VFR. ATC to IFR only; IFR separated from IFR. Traffic info to all *as far as practical*. **Not used for control zones.**
**F**: IFR + VFR. IFR get advisory service; all get flight information service if requested.
**G**: IFR + VFR. Flight information service if requested.
Memory: A "Alone IFR", then separation drops: A,B all separated -> C: IFR sep from all -> D: IFR sep from IFR only -> E: IFR controlled only -> F: advisory -> G: info only.
== Who gets ATC service?
(a) All IFR in classes A, B, C, D, E. (b) All VFR in B, C, D. (c) All special VFR. (e) All aerodrome traffic at controlled aerodromes.
== Other rules
**OCA**: airspace above **FL55** over oceans (e.g. North Atlantic) is designated oceanic control area with strict rules and special navigation procedures.
**SVFR**: any airspace class that supports a CTR permits SVFR.
**Non-radio**: classes **E-G** allow VFR traffic without VHF two-way radio.
**Speed limit**: **250 kt IAS** for VFR in class C and all traffic in D-G, **below 10 000 ft (FL100)** only.
== India specifics
India uses ATS airspace classes **D, E, F, G** (per NOTAM G 0066/99). Delhi and Mumbai are Class D.
D vs E in India: in D, traffic info is given for all IFR & VFR; in E, traffic info only where possible.
== Holding speeds (ICAO max)
Up to 14 000 ft: **230 kt** | 14 000-20 000: **240 kt** | 20 000-34 000: **265 kt** | Above 34 000: **M0.83**.
Approach plate chart numbering: ILS = 11-1, VOR = 13-1, NDB = 16-1 (second airport in the same city, e.g. Bangalore: ILS 21-1, VOR 23-1).
IAS switches to Mach at **FL270**.
Q: Describe Class A to G in one line each.
A: A: IFR only, all separated. B: IFR+VFR, all separated. C: IFR+VFR, IFR separated from all, VFR from IFR only. D: IFR+VFR, IFR sep from IFR; traffic info on VFR; VFR gets traffic info on all. E: IFR controlled/separated from IFR, traffic info to all where practical, not for CTRs. F: advisory to IFR, FIS on request. G: FIS on request.
T: A = Absolute control; G = Go-your-own-way. Each step down loses some separation service.
Q: Which airspace classes are used in India?
A: D, E, F and G (NOTAM G 0066/99). Delhi and Mumbai are Class D.
Q: Difference between Class D and E in India?
A: D: traffic info for all IFR and VFR. E: traffic info only where possible.
Q: Which airspaces provide ATC service?
A: IFR in A-E, VFR in B-D, all special VFR, all aerodrome traffic at controlled aerodromes.
QN: Speed limit in airspace?
A: 250 kt IAS for VFR in class C and all traffic in D-G, applicable only below 10 000 ft (FL100).
Q: Which classes allow VFR without two-way VHF radio?
A: Classes E to G.
QN: What is an OCA and where does it start?
A: Oceanic Control Area - airspace above FL55 over oceans, with strict rules and special navigation procedures.
QN: ICAO maximum holding speeds?
A: Up to 14 000 ft: 230 kt. 14 000-20 000: 240 kt. 20 000-34 000: 265 kt. Above 34 000: M0.83.
T: 230 - 240 - 265 - M0.83: rises 10, then 25, then switches to Mach.
Q: What is the chart code for ILS, VOR and NDB on approach plates?
A: ILS 11-1, VOR 13-1, NDB 16-1. For a second airport in the same city: ILS 21-1, VOR 23-1.
QN: At what level does IAS change to Mach?
A: FL270.
Q: Can Class E be used for control zones?
A: No.

# 5. Communication Failure
== First reaction
Try to re-establish contact with the appropriate ATC unit using **all other available means**. Where **CPDLC** exists, voice communication must still be maintained.
How ATC confirms comm failure: asks you to do a **specific manoeuvre observable on radar**, or **transmit a signal** to acknowledge. ATC also transmits **blind** on frequencies where you're believed to be listening.
Squawk **7600** = radio failure.
== In VMC (whatever your flight rules)
Continue in VMC, land at the **nearest suitable aerodrome**, report arrival by the most expeditious means. If advisable, you may complete the IFR flight.
== In IMC - the timeline
**Non-radar airspace**: maintain last assigned speed and level (or minimum flight altitude if higher) for **20 minutes** after failing to report over a compulsory reporting point, then adjust to the filed flight plan.
**Radar airspace**: maintain last assigned speed/level (or MFA if higher) for **7 minutes** after the *latest of*:
- (a) the last assigned level/MFA reached, or
- (b) transponder set to **7600**, or
- (c) failure to report over a compulsory reporting point.
Then adjust to the filed flight plan.
**Descent**: start at the nav aid/fix at or as close as possible to the **expected approach time (EAT)** last received and acknowledged; if none, at the **ETA from the current flight plan**.
**Landing**: if possible, within **30 minutes after the ETA**.
Q: What do you do on communications failure?
A: Try all other means to contact ATC; keep voice comms where CPDLC exists. Squawk 7600. Then follow VMC/IMC procedures.
Q: Comm failure in VMC?
A: Continue in VMC, land at the nearest suitable aerodrome, report arrival to ATC by the quickest means. May complete IFR flight if considered advisable.
QN: Comm failure in IMC - how long do you hold last assigned speed/level?
A: Non-radar: 20 minutes after missing a compulsory report point. Radar: 7 minutes after the latest of: reaching last assigned level, setting 7600, or missing a compulsory report. Then follow filed flight plan.
T: 20 for nothing, 7 for radar - "Radar = 7, Rest = 20".
Q: When do you start descent for approach after comm failure in IMC?
A: At the nav aid/fix at or close to the last acknowledged EAT; if none, at or close to ETA from the current flight plan. Land if possible within 30 min of ETA.
Q: How does ATC confirm a comm failure?
A: By asking you to perform a manoeuvre visible on radar or send a signal acknowledging; ATC also transmits blind instructions.

# 6. EDTO
== From ETOPS to EDTO
**ETOPS** originally let twin-engine aircraft fly routes previously restricted to 3-4 engine types, with a very high safety level. It became **EDTO (Extended Diversion Time Operations)** and covers **two or more engines**. The intent: **avoid a diversion, and if one happens, make sure it is safe.**
**EDTO definition**: any operation by an aeroplane with two or more turbine engines where diversion time to an en-route alternate is **greater than the threshold time** set by DGCA.
Needs DGCA approval for aeroplanes with 2+ engines or AUW > 5700 kg beyond the threshold.
== Key terms
**EDTO entry point**: first point on the route of an EDTO flight, found using the one-engine-inoperative cruise speed beyond the threshold from an en-route alternate.
**EDTO critical fuel**: fuel to fly to an en-route alternate considering, at the most critical point, the most limiting system failure (engine failure, decompression, or both).
**Maximum diversion time**: max allowable range, expressed in time, from a point on a route to an en-route alternate.
**Threshold time**: range (in time) to an en-route alternate beyond which EDTO approval is required.
Take-off and/or destination aerodromes may be treated as en-route alternates.
== DGCA threshold times
- **60 min**: twin-engine aeroplanes over **45 360 kg**, and all scheduled operators with twins regardless of weight/pax.
- **90 min**: non-scheduled operators with aeroplanes under 45 360 kg.
- **120 min**: aeroplanes with more than two engines.
== Critical fuel scenarios - take the GREATER of 3
a. **Rapid decompression** at the most critical point, descent to a safe altitude (oxygen limits).
b. Decompression **+ simultaneous engine failure** at the most critical point, at approved OEI cruise speed.
c. **Engine failure** at the most critical point, descent to OEI cruise altitude at OEI cruise speed.
== Approval experience ladder
**75/90 min**: can be granted with minimal or no in-service experience with the airframe-engine combination.
**Up to 120 min**: min **12 consecutive months** in-service experience with that airframe-engine combination.
**Up to 180 min**: must have held 120 min EDTO approval for at least **12 months** with high demonstrated propulsion reliability.
== Adequate vs suitable
**Adequate airfield**: long enough runway, sufficiently equipped - ATC, lighting, comms, weather reporting, navaids, safety cover available at time of use.
**Suitable airfield**: commander satisfies himself on the day, using operator criteria, that adequate airfields - considering weather and equipment unserviceabilities - are suitable for the intended operation.
T: Adequate = on paper (facilities). Suitable = on the day (weather + serviceability).
Q: What is EDTO?
A: Extended Diversion Time Operations - any operation by an aeroplane with two or more turbine engines where the diversion time to an en-route alternate exceeds the DGCA threshold time.
Q: How has ETOPS evolved to EDTO?
A: ETOPS was for twins on routes previously for 3-4 engine aircraft. EDTO covers two or more engines. Intent: avoid diversion, and ensure a safe one if it happens.
QN: DGCA threshold times?
A: 60 min - twins above 45 360 kg and all scheduled-operator twins. 90 min - non-scheduled under 45 360 kg. 120 min - more than two engines.
Q: What is EDTO critical fuel?
A: Fuel needed to reach an en-route alternate considering the most limiting system failure (engine failure, decompression or both) at the most critical point. Take the greatest of: decompression; decompression + engine failure; engine failure.
Q: Adequate vs suitable airfield?
A: Adequate: long enough runway and necessary ancillary services (ATC, lighting, comms, weather, navaids, rescue). Suitable: on the day, commander confirms adequate aerodromes are suitable given weather and equipment unserviceabilities.
QN: Experience required for 120 and 180 min EDTO?
A: 120 min: 12 consecutive months in-service with that airframe-engine. 180 min: 12 months holding a 120 min approval with high propulsion reliability. 75/90 min: minimal or no experience needed.
Q: Can take-off or destination be used as en-route alternates for EDTO?
A: Yes.
Q: What is EDTO entry point?
A: The first point on an EDTO route, determined using OEI cruise speed, beyond the threshold time from an en-route alternate.
